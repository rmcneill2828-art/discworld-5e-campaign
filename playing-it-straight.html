GM Craft Guide

Playing It Straight

Nine techniques for running comedy and satire the way the books do — and a checklist for the table.

Companion to The Discworld Charter

Nobody in Ankh-Morpork thinks they're funny. That's the whole trick.

Discworld's comedy almost never comes from characters cracking jokes — it comes from the gap between how seriously everyone in the world takes their absurd situation and how clearly the reader can see the absurdity. The moment a GM starts winking at the table, or an NPC starts lampshading how ridiculous everything is, that gap closes and the joke dies with it. Everything below is about keeping that gap open: play every character, every institution, and every catastrophe with complete sincerity, and let the humor live entirely in the premise.

Each technique below comes with a quick before/after — a generic fantasy line on the left, the same beat run through the technique on the right. Steal the rights-hand column directly if you need something at the table tonight.

1

Make the Abstract Literal

Take a concept a normal fantasy setting would leave metaphorical — belief, time, magic, inspiration, story logic itself — and give it a physical, measurable, occasionally bureaucratic reality. Death isn't a metaphor here; he has a house, a horse, and a filing system.

Generic

"An overwhelming sense of destiny settled over her."

Discworld

"Narrativium settled over her, the way it does over anyone about to do something a story needs. She could feel it between her shoulder blades, like a hand deciding where to push."

2

Bureaucracy Meets the Fantastic

Apply mundane institutional process — forms, licenses, unions, insurance, filing — to epic-fantasy events. The dragon is terrifying; the dragon is also, according to a small brass plaque, Grade II listed. This is the single most reliable engine in the whole toolkit, and it works in almost any scene.

Generic

"The ancient dragon reared up, ready to raze the village."

Discworld

"The dragon was ancient, terrible, and — according to the plaque on its cave — a Scheduled Monument, which meant slaying it required a permit from the Ankh-Morpork Historical Preservation Society, obtainable Tuesdays, in person, with a fee."

3

Subvert the Trope, Don't Mock the Genre

Discworld pokes at fantasy clichés without ever sneering at the reader for enjoying them. The prophecy is real and it does matter — it's just been misprinted, or it fits three people at once, or the chosen one would really rather not. Keep the stakes genuine even while the trope gets undercut.

Generic

"You are the chosen one, prophesied to save the realm."

Discworld

"The prophecy says a chosen one will save the realm, and that they'll bear a birthmark shaped like a teapot. Awkwardly, three people in this village have that birthmark, and one of them's currently wanted for tax fraud."

4

The Narrator's Voice

When you narrate as GM, keep your own voice dry and matter-of-fact even at the most absurd moments — let the situation be funny while your delivery stays level. Never editorialize with "and that was pretty silly" energy. State the ridiculous thing the same way you'd state the weather.

Generic

"Weirdly, the wizard's hat starts floating — kind of a funny moment, honestly."

Discworld

"The wizard's hat rises eleven inches off his head and stays there. He doesn't react. This has, apparently, happened before."

5

Escalating Specificity

Lists and enumerations that start reasonable and end absurd are one of the setting's most reliable rhythms. Three items, the first two grounding, the third one breaking the pattern — in dialogue, signage, item descriptions, anywhere you'd otherwise write one flat sentence.

Generic

"The market sold food, cloth, and other goods."

Discworld

"The market sold fish, secondhand boots, and a man who would, for a small fee, personally guarantee your fortune for the day."

6

Undercut the Epic — Sparingly

A dramatic confrontation resolved by something petty or mundane is a real technique, not just a gag — but it only lands when you've earned the drama first, and it shouldn't happen every session. Save it for the moment the table is braced for something huge.

Generic

"The Dark Lord fell, and the age of shadow ended forever."

Discworld

"The Dark Lord fell. It took eleven minutes to establish this was, legally, an accident, and another two hours to work out who was now on the hook for his outstanding tab at the Mended Drum."

Use sparingly If every big moment gets deflated, the table stops investing in big moments at all. Let some prophecies land straight, some battles matter exactly as much as they're supposed to — the anticlimax works because it's the exception.
7

Names, Signs, and Institutions

Names carry the joke by themselves — the text never needs to point at them. A shop sign, a Guild motto, a street name can do a scene's worth of world-building in one line. Write them completely straight; the humor is in the reader noticing, not in anyone in-world remarking on it.

  • Alterations While-U-Wait (Wait May Exceed Lifetime)
  • The Worshipful Company of Rope-Makers — Est. Sometime Before Anyone Complained
  • Genuine Antique Grimoires. Curses Not Included. Ask About Curses.
  • We Bury Our Mistakes, and Yours, Same Rate
  • Fresh Fish. Ish.
  • Nothing Locked, Nothing Guaranteed
8

Satire With a Heart

Pratchett's satire has a target, and it's consistent: institutions, pretension, cruelty dressed up as tradition, power that's stopped noticing the people underneath it. It is not aimed at the poor, the frightened, the ordinary person just trying to get through the week — those characters are usually the ones the narrative actually respects. The cynic in the room (Vimes, Granny Weatherwax) is almost always covering for real moral seriousness underneath.

Before a joke lands on a character, ask who it's actually laughing at. Aim it at the guild that's forgotten what it's for, the noble who's never worked a day, the system that's stopped making sense — not at the constable walking a beat, or the beggar working a patch, or anyone in the room who's just trying to get by.

9

What Breaks the Voice

  • Fourth-wall winks. An NPC saying "well, that's convenient, isn't it" kills the gap the whole style depends on. Let the absurdity sit there, uncommented on, in-world.
  • Slapstick as the whole joke. Pratfalls happen, but they're rarely the punchline by themselves — they usually arrive already wrapped in a bureaucratic, institutional, or linguistic joke doing the real work.
  • Cynicism without warmth. The setting can be bleak and often is — but nihilism for its own sake reads as a different, colder book. There's almost always a person underneath the joke worth rooting for.
  • Punching down. Save the satire for power, pretension, and institutions. A joke that lands on someone powerless just isn't a Discworld joke, however it's dressed up.
  • Joke density with no stakes. A scene that's wall-to-wall gags with nothing genuinely at risk stops feeling like the books fast. The comedy works because the danger is real.

Table Quick Reference

  • Deliver the absurd thing exactly as flatly as you'd deliver the weather.
  • When in doubt, give it a form, a fee, a licensing body, or a union.
  • Let the joke live in the premise and the name — never point at it out loud.
  • Keep the danger real. The comedy needs the stakes to work.
  • Aim satire at power and pretension, never at anyone just getting by.
  • Save the anticlimax for the one moment the table's genuinely braced.

None of this replaces good instincts at the table — it's scaffolding for the nights your instincts want a nudge. Read a chapter before a session if you need to recalibrate the voice; it comes back fast.
