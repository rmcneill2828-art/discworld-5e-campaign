The Idea

Nobody in Ankh-Morpork has ever said "I just hit level six." They said "I made Corporal."

Your character's numeric level is untouched — it's still what sets your proficiency bonus, spell slots, and HP, earned exactly the way your table already earns levels. What changes is the name for it. Instead of "Level 6," you hold a rank in your Origin's ladder — Corporal, Journeyman, Bachelor of Arts, whatever your Guild or institution calls it at that stage.

Here's the twist that makes it more than a relabel: rank doesn't rise automatically with level. Your level sets the ceiling you're eligible for — actually holding that rank means it was earned in the fiction, through a Promotion. That gap between the two is where the interesting characters live: a First-Year Student at Unseen University who is unmistakably too old, too capable, and too dangerous to still be attending remedial thaumaturgy with seventeen-year-olds, because for whatever reason — a late calling, a ruined first career, a bet — that's the rank they're actually holding.

"You'll forgive me," said the proctor, not looking up from the enrollment scroll, "but you do understand this is the first-year practical."
"I understand."
"You're aware most of the other students are seventeen."
"I'm aware."
The proctor finally looked up. "Well," he said. "Try not to make it weird."

How Promotion Works

  1. Eligibility. Each rank in a ladder lists a minimum character level. You can never hold a rank above what your current level allows — but you can absolutely sit well below it.
  2. The bid. When a player wants their character to seek the next rank, they say so — usually in downtime, or right after a story beat that plausibly demonstrates the next rank's responsibilities. Nothing happens automatically; someone has to actually try.
  3. The scene. The GM frames a short scene fitting the institution: an exam, a quota review, a sponsor's recommendation, a duel of aptitude, a peer vote, an audience with someone who can say yes. Resolve it with one meaningful roll or roleplay decision, not a mechanical subsystem.
  4. The outcome. Succeed, and the rank advances one tier — no earlier than the story's own pace allows; a scene shouldn't be re-run twice in a session. Fail, and the character stays put, free to try again once more play has passed.
No obligation to climb A player can leave a promotion bid unmade for as long as they want. Staying the overqualified First-Year, the Journeyman who never sat their Master's boards, the Constable who's turned down Sergeant twice — that's a valid character choice, not a wasted opportunity. The mismatch is the point.
Switching origins Change your Origin — thrown out of the University, recruited off the street into the Watch — and you re-enter the new ladder at its bottom rank, regardless of your level or your standing in the old one. Reputation can travel with you as a roleplay advantage at the GM's discretion, but the title never does. Everyone starts at the bottom of a new ladder; that's what makes it a ladder.

Civilian

No institution, no exam, no sponsor — reputation on the street builds itself, whether you want it to or not.

RankMin LevelNotes
Nobody in Particular1You haven't done anything anyone's noticed yet.
Known Face4Publicans remember your name. So, unfortunately, does the Watch.
Somebody8People point you out on the street. Still no Guild card.
A Name People Use13Strangers have heard of you before you introduce yourself.
Living Legend, Allegedly18Three taverns claim you drank there. You've been to one of them.

Nobody grants a Civilian rank — there's no exam, no sponsor, no promotion scene to bid for. It simply accrues from what the character has actually done, so the GM awards it directly whenever the fiction has clearly earned it. It can lag behind level exactly like every other ladder — a genuinely dangerous level-15 civilian who's kept their head down and their name off the street is a perfectly good character.

Table Notes

Building a ladder for a Guild not listed here (Musicians', Seamstresses', Beggars'): six tiers, roughly at levels 1, 3–4, 6–8, 10–13, 15–17, and 19–20, is the pattern every table above follows. Name the bottom tier after the exam or apprenticeship that gets someone in the door, and the top tier after whichever single seat the Guild only ever has one of.

This is optional, and it's layered on top of — not instead of — normal leveling. If a promotion bid never comes up at your table, nothing breaks; every character simply reads at whatever rank their level allows by default, and the ladders still work as flavor text for NPCs.

Playing the mismatch. As with the Origins system in the Charter, an out-of-band rank is a roleplay hook, never a mechanical one — a level-11 First-Year keeps every one of their level-11 features. What changes is how NPCs address them, what's assumed about their competence in a new room, and how satisfying the eventual promotion scene gets to be.
