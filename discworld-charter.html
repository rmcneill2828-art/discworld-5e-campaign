I

Premise & Tone

The Disc is a fully-formed fantasy world that has already read every fantasy novel and finds most of it faintly ridiculous — and that's the game you're running.

Nothing about the rules needs to change to run this. 5e's classes, races, and math already model "a person with a sword," "a person with spells," and "a person who talks their way out of trouble" perfectly well. What changes is the lens: the prophecy turns out to be a misprint, the ancient evil has a union rep, and the hero's real problem after the dragon is the paperwork.

Run it as sincere fantasy that happens to notice its own tropes, not as parody that undercuts every scene. Pratchett's satire works because the characters take their world completely seriously — Vimes really does hate crime, Granny Weatherwax really is that powerful, Death really does care. Let your table play it straight and let the setting supply the irony. The companion Playing It Straight breaks this down into nine concrete techniques with before/after rewrites, if you want the craft spelled out.

*Great A'Tuin's sex has never been established. Sages have theorized for centuries. Do not let this hold up your session zero.
II

Cosmology

The Disc is flat, roughly circular, and rides through space on the backs of four elephants standing on the shell of Great A'Tuin, a star turtle swimming an unknown course. The Hub (center, where magic pools thickest, crowned by the ten-mile spire of Cori Celesti) and the Rim (edge, where the ocean genuinely does fall off into space) are your compass points instead of north and south.

Magic is a physical force

Magic on the Disc is real, measurable, and visible as octarine — an eighth primary color, a fluorescing greenish-purple only wizards, witches, and cats can see. It behaves a little like a fluid: it can be stored, drained, and overloaded. A handful of people (an eighth son of an eighth son of an eighth son) are born sourcerers, wired directly into the Source of magic itself — treat that as a campaign-ending event, not a character option. A summoned noble dragon belongs in the same rare, story-defining tier — see Powers Worth Knowing for both, and for the far more common, far less apocalyptic swamp dragon.

Gods run on belief

Discworld deities are powered by worship, in a fairly literal economy: a god with a million believers strides the Hub in armor, and a god with none is a lizard the size of your thumb, shouting at passing beetles in the desert. This makes belief itself a resource NPCs will fight over — useful for any plot involving temples, cults, or a Small God looking for a sponsor. For a working pantheon, a Small God generator, and other supernatural lore (the Auditors, the Horsemen, Fairyland's elves), see Powers Worth Knowing.

Death, and the Auditors

Death is a person: seven feet of black robe and bone, speaks in a voice you feel more than hear†, rides a white horse named Binky, keeps bees, and is — despite the job — one of the more decent people on the Disc. His opposite numbers are the Auditors of Reality, a grey hive-mind bureaucracy that finds individual life tasteless and would much prefer the universe ran on schedule with no one in it. They make excellent slow-burn antagonists precisely because they never raise their voice.

†Render Death's dialogue in the text as small capitals if your table is reading room-voice out loud — it's an easy, table-tested way to make one NPC instantly recognizable without a costume.

IV

Guilds & Factions

Ankh-Morpork's defining civic idea: legalize and regulate the crime instead of pretending you can end it. Every Guild below can double as a 5e background, a patron, or a rival.

Major factions
FactionWhat they do
City WatchOnce a joke, now the closest thing the city has to real law, under Commander Sam Vimes. Multi-species by policy — dwarfs, trolls, a werewolf, a golem have all worn the badge.
Thieves' GuildLicensed theft with an enforced annual quota per citizen. Stealing without a license is the actual crime.
Assassins' GuildAn elite, expensive finishing school that also kills people for money — client pays for an "inhumation," never a "murder." Old money, old manners, real menace.
Unseen UniversityThe wizarding college. Enormous, self-important, riddled with faculty politics, home to the Archchancellor, an orangutan Librarian who is extremely clear he is not a monkey, and a thinking-engine called Hex.
Witches of the RamtopsNot a guild at all — deliberately no hierarchy, no hall, no dues. Cover their territory through reputation, favors, and "headology": solving problems by understanding people rather than transforming them.
Fools' GuildJesters must be licensed and their jokes pre-approved. Notoriously humorless in person. Excellent comic-relief patron or rival.
Alchemists' GuildExperiments reliably end in structural damage. A reliable source of volatile magic items and insurance-claim plot hooks.
Merchants' GuildTrade, tariffs, and civic prosperity — the Guild that keeps the city solvent, and quietly assumes that makes it the one that matters most.
Beggars' GuildLicensed poverty, patch by patch, street by street. Territory disputes are settled by seniority, not force — mostly.

Every faction above has a full 5e Background writeup in the companion Guild Registration Dossiers, and a promotion ladder in The Long Climb.

V

Magic Systems

Keep every spell's mechanics exactly as written in the Player's Handbook. What you're changing is the description a player reads aloud and the color a GM narrates when it resolves.

Wizards — Unseen University

Reflavor the Wizard class as an Unseen University education: a spellbook is a grimoire chained to a lectern, components are increasingly baroque apparatus, and the eight orders of wizardry (Conjurors, Diviners, Enchanters, Invokers, Magickers, Sorcerers, Thaumaturgists, Illusionists) make good flavor for a player's chosen specialty. Every UU student learns some form of Rule One early — the gist of it is do not meddle with things you don't understand — and it isn't classroom caution for its own sake. Mechanically nothing changes — this is entirely narration.

The actual reason: heavy, careless, or overloaded magic doesn't just risk a spell going wrong — it thins the boundary between the Disc and the Dungeon Dimensions, a cold, hostile nowhere-space that's hungry for anything with real substance. This is the setting's built-in reason for wizards to be cautious rather than reckless, and it's a GM tool worth having on hand for any scene involving big magic. Full lore, a named example, and an escalation table for building that dread live in Powers Worth Knowing.

Witches — headology, not spells

Witches work best as a reflavored Druid, or a Wisdom-forward Cleric, rather than a literal Wizard. Their most iconic ability, Borrowing (a witch's mind riding along inside an animal, leaving her body behind), maps cleanly onto Wild Shape's narrative shell or a ritual-cast animal messenger/speak with animals. Encourage witch players to solve problems with Insight, Persuasion, and a good bluff before reaching for a spell slot — that's the class working as intended.

Priests — belief as fuel

Run Cleric RAW, but let a character's god visibly strengthen or weaken with the size and fervor of its following — a good lever for the GM, optional for players. Channel Divinity becomes "the god notices you," which reads differently for a priest of a major Hub deity versus a priest of a Small God still building a congregation.

Optional table — casting mishap When a spellcaster rolls a natural 1 on an attack roll tied to a spell, let the player draw a d6 flavor result instead of a mechanical penalty: 1) smell of ozone and burnt wool, 2) every octarine light in the room flickers, 3) nearby cats hiss and scatter, 4) the spell works but a full minute late, 5) a passing wizard three streets over gets a headache, 6) nothing visible happens, which is somehow worse. Pure narration — the spell's actual effect is unchanged. If a scene has had several of these in a row, or the party's been leaning on genuinely heavy magic, reach for Powers Worth Knowing's "Signs the Walls Are Thinning" table instead — the same idea, with real stakes behind it.

VII

Origins: Guild, Watch, or Civilian

At character creation, every player answers one question before anything else: who backs you up when it goes wrong?

The choice is a Guild (Thieves', Assassins', Alchemists', Merchants', Fools', Beggars', or another of your own), the City Watch, Unseen University, or nobody — you're a civilian, poor or otherwise unaffiliated, and the story is what happens to ordinary people when extraordinary things keep landing on them.

Mechanically, this is just the 5e Background slot, reflavored. It was always independent of class — you're simply choosing which of the Disc's institutions issued yours, rather than treating Background as an afterthought.

Origin, built on the existing Background system
OriginBackground baseWhat it grants
City WatchSoldier or Guard, reflavored "Watchman"A badge, standing to investigate, and Vimes' personal scrutiny the moment you embarrass the uniform.
A GuildCriminal (Thieves'/Assassins'), Guild Artisan, or a custom Guild backgroundA license, a hall to fall back on, and dues or a quota you're now on the hook for.
Unseen UniversitySage or Acolyte, reflavored student / porter / hanger-onLibrary and workshop access, and a front-row seat to faculty politics.
CivilianFolk Hero, Urchin, Hermit, or similarTotal freedom — no dues, no license, no institutional cover when the Watch asks questions.

Class and origin never gate each other

This is the whole point, and it's straight out of the books: nothing on the Disc stops a Fighter from enrolling at Unseen University, a Cleric from joining the Thieves' Guild, or a Wizard from walking a Watch beat. A troll made captain, a golem earned citizenship, a teetotal vampire took up crime photography, a zombie ran for a seat on the council — the setting's whole argument is that competence and persistence beat "type" every time.

So keep it simple at your table: no class ever loses features, spells, or proficiencies for a mismatched origin. A Fighter at Unseen University keeps full Fighter chassis, full stop.

What actually changes on a mismatch Not the character sheet — the room. A Fighter enrolled at Unseen University gets treated as muscle-for-hire by faculty who won't remember their name, has to fight for a straight answer in a library that assumes everyone reads Old High Nomic, and probably ends up everyone's bodyguard whether they wanted the job or not. A Wizard on Watch patrol gets side-eyed for showing up to a stakeout in a pointy hat, and has to earn the same trust a beat cop earns by walking the same beat twice. Play the friction as raised eyebrows, extra paperwork, and an uphill climb for institutional trust — never as a mechanical tax. It's a roleplay hook, and often the best one at the table.

Full stat blocks for each origin — skills, tools, equipment, and a feature — live in the companion Guild Registration Dossiers. For tables who want rank and seniority to matter, each Origin also has its own promotion ladder in The Long Climb — an optional system where your title can lag behind your level.

Every character starts in the city itself, for reasons and by routes best left to The Skull Dice — a pair of d20 tables for why you're in Ankh-Morpork and how you got there. For the whole process in one place, see First Day in the City, a one-page character creation quick-start.

IX

Running It RAW

The whole point of this charter is that you shouldn't need to touch the rulebook math. A short checklist for staying RAW while still feeling like the Disc:

  • Reflavor, don't rebuild. Rename spells, items, and class features to match the setting; leave every number untouched.
  • Origin and class stay uncoupled. As above — a mismatched pairing changes the roleplay, never the character sheet.
  • Push consequences into narration. The casting-mishap table earlier is the pattern — flavor color on a bad roll, never a hidden mechanical tax.
  • Pace by book, not by dungeon. Structure arcs the way the novels do: one contained civic or personal crisis per "book," milestone leveling at the end of each, rather than a strict XP-per-encounter grind.
  • Track faction standing informally. A line in your notes for "Watch," "Guild," and "Patrician" opinion of the party goes a long way before you need a formal reputation subsystem.
  • Let Vetinari be the DM's best tool. A patient, terrifyingly well-informed authority figure who never needs to raise his voice is the cheapest way to keep a sandbox campaign pointed somewhere.

For combat specifically, the same rule applies twice as hard: Threats Worth Naming points a set of Discworld dangers at unmodified 5e statblocks, so you're never inventing new math mid-session.

X

Adventure Hooks

Original, book-agnostic seeds — nothing here retells a specific novel's plot, so they're safe to drop into a home campaign without spoiling anything.

  • Ankh-MorporkA newly-arrived noble family's pet swamp dragon has gone missing in the Shades, and it's still growing.
  • Assassins' GuildA student's final exam contract has gone wrong, and the intended target has hired the party to find out who's still coming.
  • LancreSomething is realigning the old standing stones outside a Ramtops village, and the local witch wants outside eyes on it before she commits to a response.
  • Thieves' GuildAn unlicensed operator is undercutting the Guild's theft quota, and the Guild would rather hire quiet investigators than start a turf war.
  • Unseen UniversityA faculty experiment has gone missing from a locked storeroom and is, worryingly, still on the payroll.
  • The PalaceLord Vetinari has a "small errand" that is transparently a test, and everyone involved knows it, and no one can afford to say no.
XI

Closing Note

This charter draws on general knowledge of the Discworld series rather than any single book's text — if you want a deep dive grounded in a specific title (Lancre's geography as it appears across Wyrd Sisters, Witches Abroad, and Carpe Jugulum, say, or the exact structure of the Watch across the City Watch arc), point me at the book and I can work from the source directly.

Treat everything above as a starting charter, not a finished law. Discworld's own running joke is that its geography and rules firmed up under pressure, in play, one book at a time — your table gets to do the same.
