Peoples of the Disc

By Choice, Not Nature

Zombies, Black-Ribboner vampires, and werewolves — three ways of staying a person when something in you argues otherwise.

The Charter's species table groups these three under one row — "Reformed undead" — with Angua and Otto offered as the model. That's the right instinct; here's the depth behind it. All three are defined less by what they are than by an ongoing, active choice to stay part of civilized life instead of the alternative their nature offers.

Zombies

How it happens

Rarely a ritual, and almost never a choice made in advance. Most zombies arise through magical resurrection gone slightly sideways, occasional divine intervention — or, most often and most Pratchett of all, simple bureaucratic oversight: Death fails to collect a soul before the body technically expires, and the person in question just... keeps going. Nobody signs up for this. They just don't stop.

Mind and body

A Discworld zombie is nothing like the mindless kind — full mental faculties are retained: thinking, planning, scheming, holding a conversation, holding a job. There's a real in-world theory that death strips away enough illusion to leave a zombie unusually clear-headed, even sharper in some ways than they were alive. What doesn't come back is healing — a wound can be stitched, but it won't close on its own, and decomposition proceeds regardless, at whatever pace it proceeds.

A living, working status

Legal and social recognition is real but incomplete — zombies hold down genuine jobs (a meticulous lawyer, an unflappable obituary writer are both exactly the kind of hire a zombie's own clarity of mind makes sense for), while full personhood remains a live, ongoing argument. Names Worth Knowing's Reg Shoe is that argument's activist end, already in this repo.

GM Tip The zombie's real "choice" isn't over blood or moonlight — it's the daily insistence on remaining a person with a job, a name, and opinions, instead of quietly becoming just a corpse that happens to still be moving. Play that insistence as quietly heroic, not grim.

Black-Ribboner Vampires

The League of Temperance

Black Ribboners have signed the pledge of the Uberwald League of Temperance (with its own Ankh-Morpork chapter now serving the city's black-caped immigrants) — a self-help movement built on the same structure as any real-world sobriety program: mutual support, a public pledge, and a word for having kept it. A vampire fully off human blood is B-total.

The switch

Sobriety doesn't remove a vampire's obsessive drive — it redirects it. A Black Ribboner trades bloodlust for a new, equally consuming fixation: Otto Chriek, already the Charter's model example, became an iconographer, despite the small professional hazard that a bright enough flash briefly turns him to dust. That's the pattern worth using for any Black-Ribboner PC or NPC — sobriety doesn't make the intensity go away, it just needs somewhere else to go.

Sober isn't the same as safe Some Black Ribboners redirect that same obsessive energy somewhere far less wholesome than photography — ambition, control over people, obsessive perfectionism. A vampire who's genuinely never touched human blood can still be difficult, manipulative, or quietly dangerous in an entirely different register. Don't let "reformed" become shorthand for "harmless."

Relapse

Falling off the wagon is a real, serious risk, already covered as an encounter in Threats Worth Naming's Off-the-Wagon Vampire entry — the tragedy there is exactly this movement failing someone, not a twist.

Werewolves

The shift

A trained werewolf shifts by will, not just by moonlight — though full moonlight pulls hard toward wolf shape regardless of intention. A rare few, called yennorks, are locked permanently into one form or the other and can't shift even under a full moon. Human form brings hands, sight, and reason; wolf form brings claws, extraordinary scent, and sharp combat instinct — but too long spent as a wolf dulls the capacity for complex thought and social nuance, so a werewolf can't simply live as a wolf and stay who they are.

The quieter struggle

The full moon isn't the real day-to-day test. It's smaller "canine instinct" moments even in full human control — the pull to chase a thrown ball, a bolting suspect, a fleeing cat — that a werewolf has to consciously override, constantly, without anyone else in the room necessarily noticing the effort. Angua, already in Names Worth Knowing, is written exactly this way — "permanently negotiating with her own nature" is the whole idea, not an exaggeration.

Society and vulnerability

Uberwald werewolves organize as large feudal clans rather than single noble houses, with old, traditional rivalries against vampire Houses and each other. They're otherwise nearly immortal — only fire or silver cause lasting harm, and a silver collar can trap a werewolf in wolf form specifically, a real and genuinely cruel plot device rather than just a weakness on a stat block.

What Ties Them Together

None of these three are costumes or monsters-of-the-week here — they're people making an active, ongoing choice to stay part of civilized life against something that would define them otherwise, every single day, not just once at an origin story. A zombie chooses to remain a person and not just a corpse that kept moving. A vampire and a werewolf choose control over instinct, daily, the same way anyone maintains any hard-won discipline.

GM Tip Never let "reformed" read as "boring" or "safe." The ongoing effort of staying reformed is the character — and a moment of real temptation, or a lapse, is always the most dramatically honest beat available to any of these three, played with weight rather than as a jump-scare twist.
